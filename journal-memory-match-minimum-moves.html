Memory Match minimum moves: what is a good score for 6, 8 or 12 pairs?

You finish a Memory Match board in 19 moves. Is that good? The answer depends on how many pairs you played and what the game counts as a move. Here is a simple way to understand your result, set a fair target and improve without rushing.

Play Memory Match →

What counts as one move in Mindora?

In Mindora Memory Match, one move is one attempt with two different cards. Flipping the first card alone does not increase the counter. Once you reveal a second card, the game adds one move, whether the symbols match or not.

If the cards match, they stay visible. If they do not, the two cards are hidden again after a short delay of about 850 milliseconds. The counter does not go down when you find a pair.

Worked example: three attempts

  1. Flip the moon and the fox: no match. The counter reads 1 move.
  2. Flip the moon and the moon: match. The counter reads 2 moves, and that pair stays revealed.
  3. Flip the fox and the fox: match. The counter reads 3 moves.

The three attempts used six card reveals and found two pairs. You can have more moves than pairs because unsuccessful attempts still count.

The mathematical minimum for each difficulty

Every pair must be found through a two-card attempt. Therefore, if you match every attempt successfully, the smallest possible number of moves equals the number of pairs. This is a theoretical minimum, not a typical first-try result: a freshly shuffled board starts with hidden card identities, so you normally need exploratory attempts to learn where the matches are.

For example, a 14-move finish on Medium means eight successful attempts and six unsuccessful ones. On Hard, 14 moves would mean twelve successful attempts and only two unsuccessful ones. The same number of moves tells a very different story at different difficulties.

Compare scores fairly: count extra moves

A simple personal benchmark is extra moves = total moves minus pairs. This tells you how many attempts did not produce a match. It does not claim to measure memory ability or compare you with other players.

Suppose you finish Easy in 11 moves. That is 11 - 6 = 5 extra moves. If your next Easy round takes 9 moves, you made two fewer unsuccessful attempts. Compare rounds at the same difficulty because the number of hidden pairs changes.

Three practical ways to cut extra moves

  1. Name the location when you see a symbol. Think “fox: top row, second card” instead of just “fox”. On a four-column board, row-and-column labels are easier to recall than vague left/right positions.
  2. Use known locations immediately. If your first card reveals a symbol you have seen before, use the second flip to find its known partner rather than opening an unrelated unknown card.
  3. Review your misses, not just your final score. After a round, ask which symbol positions you forgot. Then try one more round on the same difficulty with a single focus: remember the first two rows, or recall one unmatched symbol at a time.

What about the timer and personal best?

Mindora offers an untimed option as well as timed rounds. The clock starts when you flip your first card. If a timed round runs out, the board is disabled and you can restart or choose a longer round. Start untimed while learning locations; use a timer later if you want an additional challenge.

The game records your lowest completed move count for each difficulty on the current day in browser storage. A new shuffle creates a different arrangement, so an improved score is useful as a personal goal, not proof that two boards were equally difficult. Clearing browser storage or changing devices can affect saved records.

Try a three-round experiment

Round 1: Choose Easy and play normally. Write down your total moves and calculate extra moves.

Round 2: Stay on Easy. Say each revealed symbol's position to yourself. Aim to reduce extra moves, not to finish faster.

Round 3: Move to Medium only if you can reliably remember locations on Easy. Compare your extra-move count with your earlier Medium attempts, not your Easy score.

Remember: a lower move count is the goal of this experiment. The game does not guarantee that every shuffled board has the same difficulty.
