Sudoku naked pairs: how two cells can unlock a whole row

You have checked every row and box, but no cell has only one possible digit. Before guessing, look for a naked pair: two empty cells in the same row, column or 3×3 box that have exactly the same two candidates. Those two digits must occupy those two cells in some order. That means no other cell in that unit can use either digit.

This is a useful next step after the single-candidate techniques in our beginner Sudoku guide. You can try it in Mindora Sudoku with pencil notes.

What counts as a naked pair?

Suppose two cells in row 4 can each contain only 2 or 7. One must be 2 and the other must be 7, even though you do not yet know which is which. Because a Sudoku row uses each digit once, every other cell in row 4 must exclude 2 and 7.

Important: the pair must be the complete candidate set for both cells. If one cell could contain 2, 7 or 9, it is not a naked pair. Also, the two cells must share the unit where you want to eliminate candidates.

A worked candidate example

Imagine that you have correctly calculated these candidates for four empty cells in the same row. This is an illustrative candidate snapshot, not a claim about a specific generated Mindora board.

CellPossible digits beforeAfter using the pair
R4C1{2, 7}{2, 7}
R4C5{2, 7}{2, 7}
R4C7{2, 5, 7}{5}
R4C9{3, 7, 9}{3, 9}

R4C7 becomes 5 because 2 and 7 are reserved for R4C1 and R4C5. You have made progress without choosing which pair cell is 2.

Why the logic is certain

  1. R4C1 has only two candidates: 2 and 7.
  2. R4C5 also has only those same two candidates.
  3. They cannot both be 2 or both be 7, because they share row 4.
  4. Therefore, together they use one 2 and one 7.
  5. Remove 2 and 7 from other unsolved cells in row 4. In this example, R4C7 is left with 5.

Do not remove 2 and 7 from unrelated cells in another row just because you found a pair in row 4. If both pair cells also share a 3×3 box, the same elimination is valid in that box too; if they do not, it is not.

How to try naked pairs in Mindora Sudoku

  1. Open Mindora Sudoku and choose Medium or Hard if you want more opportunities to use notes. The generated puzzles are not guaranteed to contain a naked pair at any particular point.
  2. Select an editable square, switch Notes on, and tap the possible digits. In Notes mode, tapping a digit adds or removes its pencil mark instead of filling the cell.
  3. Compare two-candidate cells within the same row, column or box. Look for an exact matching pair such as {2, 7} and {2, 7}.
  4. Remove those two notes from other cells in that shared unit. Pencil marks are your working notes, so you must update them yourself as the puzzle changes.
  5. When an affected cell has just one valid candidate left, switch Notes off before entering the digit.

Mindora's Check button highlights filled entries that differ from the puzzle solution; it does not certify your pencil notes. Each puzzle also provides three hints. A hint fills the selected editable square with its correct value, so save it for when you have genuinely run out of deductions.

Practice: find the useful pair

In one column, four unsolved cells have these candidate sets:

A: {1, 8} · B: {1, 4, 8} · C: {1, 8} · D: {4, 6, 8}

Question: Which two cells form a naked pair, and what happens to B and D?

Answer: A and C form the {1, 8} pair. Remove 1 and 8 from every other cell in that column: B becomes {4}, while D becomes {4, 6}. The pair does not tell you whether A is 1 or 8 yet.

Three common mistakes

  1. Matching incomplete notes: two cells marked {3, 9} are not a real pair if either still has another legal candidate that you forgot to record.
  2. Eliminating outside the shared unit: the deduction applies only to rows, columns or boxes containing both pair cells.
  3. Guessing the order: a naked pair reserves two digits but does not reveal which digit belongs in which pair cell.

What to learn next

Once you can spot naked pairs, revisit hidden singles and pencil-note basics: pair eliminations often create a new single. If you want a different kind of deduction, try the visual sequences in our Pattern Quest guide, or play a fresh round of Pattern Quest.

Try it on a real board

Start with a row or column containing several two-candidate cells. Write down candidates carefully, find one exact pair and see whether it exposes a single. If not, keep scanning instead of guessing.
